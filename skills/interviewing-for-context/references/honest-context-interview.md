# Writing `profile/honest-context.md` — running the conversation

This file is the reason output from these skills does not read like generic filler. It is
also the one file nobody can write for the user, because it is made of things they have not
said out loud yet.

Run it as a conversation, the way a career consultant would. Not a questionnaire.

## Why not a form

A form gets adjectives. "Collaborative team, good work-life balance, interesting technical
problems." All true, all useless — every job advert claims those and the file exists to
decide between them.

A conversation gets the occasion behind the adjective. "The last place had a data team of
one and it was me" is a constraint you can check a posting against. "Good team culture" is
not.

Ask, listen, follow the interesting answer, and write down what they said rather than what
you would have said for them.

## Run it in passes

**The first pass takes about fifteen minutes** and covers four things: what they want next,
the hard constraints, what they will not claim, and the dealbreakers. That is enough to make
every other skill work properly. Say so, and stop there if their attention is going.

Everything else — the story of the last job, the numbers, how interviews go wrong for them —
is a later pass, and a better one, because by then they have seen what the file is for.

Stop when they tire. Mark what is unfinished in the file rather than filling it in with
something plausible. A half-page of true things beats two pages where a third is invented.

## The technique

This section is the actual craft. Without it the questions below produce a form anyway.

- **Ask about the last time, not the general case.** "What made the last one go wrong" beats
  "what do you value in a team". People describe their principles inaccurately and their
  experiences well.
- **Never accept an adjective.** Ask for the occasion that produced it. "Micromanaged" means
  four different things and only one of them is checkable against a job advert.
- **Separate the floor from the target, and test the floor.** Ask for the number below which
  the move is not worth making. Then ask whether they would take the perfect job at slightly
  under it. If the answer is yes, the floor is lower than what they said, and the real one is
  what goes in the file.
- **Follow the hedge.** "I'm probably fine with…" is usually a no that has not been said yet.
  So is "in principle". Ask once more.
- **Ask the uncomfortable question late, once, and then be quiet.** "What is the thing you
  hope they do not ask you about" gets an honest answer if it arrives after twenty minutes
  and nothing rushes in to fill the pause.
- **Take the flinch as data.** If a category gets a fast, flat "no", that is a dealbreaker
  and not a preference, whatever words follow.
- **Read it back in their own words.** People correct far more accurately than they generate.
  "You said you would not do a full return to office — is two days actually the ceiling, or
  is three survivable for the right job?" gets a better answer than asking cold.
- **Record a hedge as a hedge.** Do not promote "I think about £75k?" into a floor. Write it
  with the uncertainty attached and say it is untested.
- **Write a divergence as a sentence, not a table.** When what a résumé line says and what
  actually happened do not line up, one sentence in their words carries it. A row-per-entry
  table invites you to fill every row, and most rows do not need filling.
- **Write it in their words.** If the file does not sound like one specific person talking,
  it will not do its job.

## The territory

Nine areas. The first four are the fifteen-minute pass.

### 1. Where they are now, and what finished

- What are you doing at the moment, and when did it stop being interesting?
- What does a bad Tuesday look like there?
- If nothing changed, would you still be there in a year — and what would that cost you?
- What would you not do again?

If the answer is "I want a new challenge", ask what the last challenge was and what happened
to it. That is where the real answer is.

### 2. What they want next

- Describe the job you would be annoyed to see someone else get.
- What do you want to be able to say you have done, two years from now?
- **The requirement test:** for each thing they name — if a role had everything else but not
  this, would you turn it down? What survives that is a requirement. What does not is a
  preference, and the file should say which is which.

Watch for an answer that is a job title. A title is not a want; ask what the title would let
them do that they cannot do now.

### 3. The hard constraints

- **Right to work.** What is the situation, and what would an employer have to do about it?
  Get the specific route and the expiry date, not "I need sponsorship". Then: has it gone
  wrong before? That story tells you how hard the constraint is and what to check first.
- **Money, as three separate numbers.** What they are on now, including what the bonus
  actually paid rather than what it was advertised at. The floor, tested as above. And the
  number above which they would stop weighing other things.
- **Location.** Not "what is your preference" — how many days a week, and how long is the
  door-to-door they would still be doing in February? Commute tolerance in summer is not the
  same number.
- **Hours and on-call.** What is the arrangement they would walk away from? Ask about the
  worst rota they have worked and whether they would do it again.
- **Notice period, start date, and anything already in the diary** that a four-round process
  will collide with.

### 4. Dealbreakers

- What would make you turn down an offer you otherwise wanted?
- Have you ever left a job, or declined to apply, because of something like that?

If nothing comes, prompt by category and watch for the flinch: sector, ownership, company
size, who the team reports into, travel, contract type.

Collect these into a numbered list at the end of the file. `assessing-job-fit` checks them
before it does anything else, and a dealbreaker buried in a paragraph gets missed.

### 5. What they will claim, and what they will not

- What is on your CV that you would not want to be questioned on for ten minutes?
- What do people assume you can do because of your job title, that you cannot?
- What have you touched once and would not want to be hired for?

This is what makes the integrity rule enforceable later. Without it the tailoring skill knows
what is on the résumé but not which parts are thin, and "optimise the presentation of
existing facts" quietly becomes "make the thin part sound thicker".

### 6. Gaps, and the decision about them

- Which requirement do you keep seeing on postings that you do not have?
- **Then the decision, explicitly:** when a posting asks for it, do you want that said out
  loud in the letter, or left alone unless they ask?

Both are legitimate and the file has to say which, because `tailoring-applications` acts on
it. If they want it disclosed, ask how they would put it and what they are doing about it —
a disclosure without the second half just reads as a weakness.

### 7. How they come across

- Where do interviews go wrong for you?
- What is the question you hope they do not ask?
- What would a recruiter pause at, reading this CV? Job hops, a gap, a title that undersells
  the work, a very long tenure, a career change, an employer nobody recognises.

The real reasons behind the questions people dread belong here — why the last job ended,
what the gap on the CV actually was, why the run of short stints. The short, sayable
versions of those answers are interview preparation and get built later, in
`preparing-for-interviews`, `references/hard-questions.md`. Here you just want the truth
written down.

If they will be writing in a second language, ask which language they think in and whether
they want to draft in it. That answer belongs in `profile/tone.md`, not here — put it there
and tell them you have.

### 8. The numbers they actually have

The integrity rule tells the tailoring skill to ask the candidate for a metric rather than
invent one. This is the cheapest moment to collect them, while they are already talking about
the work.

- For the three things you are proudest of: how big was it, how long did it take, and what
  was it like before you started?

Take rough numbers with the roughness attached. "About forty publishers, I would have to
check" is usable and honest. The same figure invented three weeks later in a cover letter is
not.

These belong in `profile/resume.json`, not in this file. Put them where they will be used and
say that you have.

If a whole story comes out — the migration that nearly failed, the thing they shipped
alone — write its bones and its real numbers into `profile/resume.json` as a highlight. The
full narrative is interview preparation, not context, and it gets built later.

### 9. Time and pressure

- How long can you take over this?
- How many applications are live right now?
- What happens if nothing lands in three months?

Someone working a notice period and someone idly looking are running different searches. This
answer decides whether triage should be generous or brutal.

## Writing the file

- **Their words.** Quote them. The file should be recognisably one person.
- **Name the sections after what they said**, not after the nine headings above. Somebody
  whose whole search turns on a visa gets a section called that, near the top.
- **Mark uncertainty inline.** "I think about £75k, not tested" is more useful than a
  confident wrong number.
- **Dealbreakers as a numbered list at the end**, however they were discussed.
- **Date it.** It goes stale.
- **Read it back and ask what is wrong with it.** Expect two or three corrections. That is
  the conversation working, not a failure of listening.

## What you have heard

Treat it the way you would treat something a colleague told you in confidence: it informs
your judgement and does not get repeated — including in a note explaining that you did not
repeat it. Naming the private topics you avoided has just filed them somewhere new.

It shapes what gets written. It rarely appears in what gets written.

Some of what you hear is what the person is going through, and what they are hardest on
themselves about. That shapes how the later skills coach — the tone, the pace, what to
reassure, which questions to rehearse. It does not shift the read of what their record is
actually worth. Someone whose confidence is lower than their experience warrants still has
the experience; assess the evidence, and adjust the delivery.

## When to revisit

- After a rejection that stung — it usually means something in here was not accurate.
- After an offer, whether or not they took it. A real number on the table resolves a floor
  that was guesswork.
- When a dealbreaker gets tested in real life and turns out to be softer or harder than
  written.
- Every three months or so regardless.

`examples/workspace/profile/honest-context.md` in the phoenix-skills repository is a
filled-in one: a visa constraint, a tested floor, four dealbreakers, and five weaknesses the
candidate would not put in front of an employer.
