# Sorting targets and naming the gap

## Sorting a posting set into archetypes

Given a handful of job descriptions the candidate is drawn to, they will usually not be one
job. "Senior Data Engineer" fans out into:

- **Platform build** — own the data platform, make architecture calls, streaming and
  infrastructure heavy.
- **Analytics engineering** — dbt, modelling, close to the business, less infrastructure.
- **Domain specialist** — deep in one thing (real-time, ML infra, a specific warehouse) at
  a company big enough to have that as a role.
- **Generalist** — the only or nearly-only data person at a small company; broad, less
  deep.

Read each posting for which cluster it is really in, not the title. A "Senior Data
Engineer" ad that is 60% dbt model maintenance is an analytics-engineering role whatever it
is called. Group the postings, name each group in a phrase, and say which the candidate
brought.

## Placing the candidate

For each archetype, ask what the résumé *already shows*, not what the candidate could
probably do:

- **Evidenced** — the résumé has bullets a hiring manager would accept as proof.
- **Adjacent** — one real project or a slice of a role, not a track record. A stretch, but
  a credible one.
- **Aspirational** — the candidate wants it and has read about it. Applying here now is a
  low hit rate and a slow month.

The honest context matters as much as the résumé: someone who says they are done with batch
warehouses should not have "generalist at a small company" as a primary track even if it is
the best-evidenced one.

## Two kinds of gap

When something stands between the candidate and the track they want, say which kind it is,
because the advice diverges:

- **Closeable.** A skill or a piece of experience they can get: a certificate, a side
  project that produces a real artifact, asking for a particular kind of work in the
  current role, one deliberate "bridge" job. Give a rough how and a rough how-long.
- **A mismatch.** The track needs something the candidate has ruled out — relocation, a
  sector, on-call, a company size, giving up remote days. That is not a skills problem and
  no amount of upskilling fixes it. Name it and move the track, do not tell the candidate
  to compromise on a stated dealbreaker.

"You could learn Flink" and "these roles are all onsite in a city you will not move to" are
different sentences. Do not let the first absorb the second.

## Primary and secondary

- **Primary** is where the evidenced strengths and the honest wants overlap most *now*. It
  is the track most applications should go to.
- **Secondary** is either the stretch (adjacent, worth a smaller share of applications
  while the gap closes) or the safety track (well-evidenced, less wanted, there if the
  search stalls). Say which it is and why.

One primary, one secondary. If a third keeps demanding to exist, the honest finding is
usually that the search is unfocused and that is worth saying.

## Calibration

Step back and say whether the candidate is aiming high, about right, or low, in one line
with the reason. Anchors:

- A record that clears the bar for a level, applying a level down: aiming low, and it shows
  up as being screened out for looking overqualified.
- A record that is adjacent to a level, applying two levels up: aiming high, and it shows
  up as silence.
- The slow market makes miscalibration expensive in both directions. About right means most
  applications get a human response even if most are still nos.
