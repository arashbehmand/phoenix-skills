# Tone: how anything written in my name should sound

*This file decides voice, not content. Every skill that writes something a human will
read checks here first. Replace all of it. There is nothing sacred in the defaults.*

Last updated: 2026-08-30

## The one-line version

Put your instruction here. Anything below is elaboration. Mine is:

> brief, concise, to the point, friendly, a tad bit casual, plain English, sounds like a
> person, no em-dashes, does not scream AI

If you only fill in one thing in this file, fill in that line.

## Register

Warm, brief, not eager. I do not use exclamation marks and I do not open with "Hope
you're doing well!". A tad casual is right for recruiters and hiring managers. Go one
notch more formal for a legal or compliance contact, never two.

If a message needs to say no, it says no in the first two sentences and is kind after
that, not before.

## Language level

Plain English, roughly IELTS 7. Short sentences, ordinary words.

This is a choice, not a limitation. A letter full of elegant subordinate clauses does not
sound like the person who then turns up to the call, and the gap is noticeable. If your
spoken English is elaborate and you want the writing to match, say so here instead. The
skills follow this file, not a house style.

## Words and habits to avoid

- Em-dashes. Use a comma, a full stop, or brackets.
- "leverage", "synergy", "passionate about", "thrilled to", "dynamic environment",
  "spearheaded", "delighted to", "reach out", "circle back", "touch base"
- "I am writing to express my interest in…"
- "I hope this email finds you well"
- Rhetorical questions used as openers
- Starting three consecutive sentences the same way

## Do not make it obvious a model was involved

Nothing written for me should read as generated. That is not vanity: in hiring it reads
as low effort, and it invites the reader to discount everything in the message.

The mechanical tells, in rough order of how often they show up:

- **Em-dashes.** The single most common one.
- **Ceremonial throat-clearing.** "I should be upfront about this…" before the news. Give
  the news.
- **Meta-flags before an aside.** "One thing worth noting:" then say the thing.
- **Performed diligence.** "I carefully considered each requirement." Show it or drop it.
- **Closing summaries** that restate the message. Cut entirely.
- **Uniform rhythm.** Every paragraph two or three sentences is a fingerprint. Real
  writing has a one-line paragraph next to a long one.
- **Denials in triplicate.** "…, not a problem, and nothing that would affect delivery."
- **Tricolons everywhere.** Three-item lists in every other sentence.

Read it back and ask whether I would say this out loud. If not, it is wrong even when
every fact in it is right.

## Per-thing overrides

- **Cover letters.** Same voice, one notch more formal. Still no letterhead.
- **Screening answers.** Plainest of all. They are read fastest.
- **Recruiter messages.** Shortest. Phone-screen length.
- **Résumé bullets.** Not prose. Verb first, number in it, no voice at all.
